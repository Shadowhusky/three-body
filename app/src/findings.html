Findings

In Liu Cixin's novel, the Trisolarans live under three suns and never know whether tomorrow brings a gentle Stable Era or a Chaotic Era that fries the planet. We took on their homework: three equal masses, Newton's law of gravity, and nothing borrowed.

324 periodic orbits

Periodic orbits are the exceptions: motions that repeat exactly, forever. A search based on time-reversal symmetry, refined with Newton's method, found 324 of them: 171 planar and 153 three-dimensional. Of the 3D ones, 28 are stable.

  • 128 of the 3D orbits are not in the largest published catalogue (Li and Liao, 2025, with 1,504 equal-mass entries). The comparison is unambiguous: orbits that are in the catalogue agree with it to 11 digits, and the rest differ from every entry by at least 3 parts in 10,000.
  • For the 27 orbits both lists share, our stable or unstable label agrees with the published one every time.
  • Twelve of the planar orbits are the well-known named ones: the figure-eight, butterflies, moths and others.

3D orbits come in families

A 3D periodic orbit is never alone. Viewed from a slowly rotating frame it belongs to a continuous family, and the orbits that close exactly are the members whose rotation per cycle is a rational fraction of a turn. Catalogues list those members one at a time. The family is the object underneath.

  • We followed the families through 144 orbits with short cycles (103 of ours, 41 from the catalogue). 60 of them belong to 20 families with several members, and 19 catalogue entries turn out to share a family with another orbit.
  • The largest family has 15 members, four of them catalogue entries. It was first computed by Michalodimitrakis in 1981; three rows of his table land on our curve to four digits.
  • Any rational rotation gives a new closed orbit, so members can be computed to order: try or . An irrational rotation gives an orbit that never closes, yet one cycle fixes it for all time. That is a Stable Era with no expiry date.

The 3D families grow out of planar orbits

Fourteen families could be followed all the way down to zero angular momentum. Thirteen of them end exactly on a planar periodic orbit we already had. The periods agree to better than one part in a million, and for most of them the initial conditions coincide as well.

One of these families leaves a planar orbit, travels through the third dimension and lands on a different planar orbit.

So the planar orbits are the skeleton of the 3D ones. For the figure-eight and the Schubart orbit this is known. For the butterfly, yin-yang and moth orbits we found no earlier statement in the papers we read.

28 orbits proved to exist

A simulation that closes to twelve digits is evidence, not proof. For 28 of the 3D orbits, validated numerics prove that an exact solution exists and is unique within 10−33 of the initial conditions we list. Interval arithmetic bounds every rounding and truncation error, and a Krawczyk test does the rest.

  • Proved: nine members of the 1981 family and nineteen orbits absent from the 2025 catalogue.
  • The method was checked on Lagrange's rotating triangle, whose exact solution it must contain, and it does. One proof repeated at higher precision certified the same solution to 40 digits.

In the novel, a calculation like this took an emperor and thirty million soldiers waving flags. We used one Mac. No sophons interfered.

A formula for the calm, a code for the chaos

  • Regular motion can be written as a sum of circles turning at steady rates. Ninety of them predict to within 0.15%, ten times beyond the span they were fitted on. Across the regular island as a whole the fit is much rougher, around 7%.
  • Chaotic motion has no such formula. What works is a code: the sequence of which body passes between the other two. Halving the distance between two initial conditions makes their codes agree for about 2.5 more symbols, so the code pins down the start like a postal address.

What did not work

A Kepler's-third-law argument predicts that the fraction of unstable triples still together at time t falls as t−2/3. We measured an exponent of −0.48 out to t = 2000. It steepens with time, but the prediction is not confirmed. Even the Trisolarans got their forecasts wrong.

  • Computer-assisted existence proofs for 28 three-dimensional orbits.
