Findings

In Liu Cixin's novel, the Trisolarans live under three suns and can never tell whether tomorrow brings a gentle Stable Era or a Chaotic Era that fries the planet. We took on their homework: three equal bodies, Newton's law of gravity, and nothing borrowed. Here is what came back.

The Trisolarans were right to dehydrate

We started three bodies 345,146 different ways and watched each one. The Map tab shows them all.

324 worlds where the calendar works

Hidden in the chaos are exact repeating dances, where the three bodies return to their starting places forever. A civilisation there could print calendars. Our search found 324 of them.

  • 171 are flat, with all three bodies in one plane. Twelve are famous ones, with names like Butterfly and Moth.
  • 153 weave through all three dimensions, like . 28 of those are stable: nudge them and they recover.
  • 128 of the three-dimensional ones do not appear in the largest published catalogue, a 2025 list of 1,504.

Proved, not just simulated

A simulation that looks right is not a proof. So for 28 of the orbits we made the computer prove they exist, using arithmetic in which every number carries a guaranteed error bound. Each true orbit sits within 0.000000000000000000000000000000001 of the numbers we list.

In the novel, a calculation like this took an emperor and thirty million soldiers waving flags. We used one Mac with a locked screen. No sophons interfered.

One string, any rhythm you order

Nine of our stable orbits turned out to be the same dance performed at different turning speeds: one continuous family. Pick any fraction of a turn and we can build the matching orbit to order. Try or .

Pick a speed that is not a fraction and the dance never repeats, yet one lap tells you where everything will be a thousand laps later. That is a Stable Era with no expiry date.

Credit where due: this family was first charted in 1981 by M. Michalodimitrakis. We found it on our own, and three rows of his table land on our curve to four digits.

A formula for the calm, a code for the chaos

  • In the calm islands, the motion can be written as a sum of circles turning at steady speeds. Ninety circles track to within 0.15%, for ten times longer than they were fitted on. Turn on “Show formula” to watch.
  • In the chaos, formulas fail completely. What works is a code: write down who passes between the other two, again and again. That string of symbols pins down the starting point like a postal address. It is the tape of coloured dots at the bottom left.

One guess that flopped

We predicted how fast doomed triples fall apart, using nothing but Kepler's third law. They turned out to hang on longer than predicted. Even the Trisolarans got their forecasts wrong, and they had nearly two hundred civilisations of practice.

What we contributed

  • An atlas anyone can fly through. A simulator built from scratch, a map of 345,146 starts, and 324 exact orbits to watch in 3D.
  • 128 three-dimensional orbits missing from the biggest catalogue. Each was checked against all 1,504 of its equal-mass entries.
  • Computer proofs that 28 orbits truly exist. They are tagged “existence proved” in the Orbits list.
  • New detail on the 1981 family. Where it begins (at a classic orbit in which the three bodies bounce along a line), how far it stays stable, and how to build members that close exactly. Four entries in the 2025 catalogue turn out to be members of it.
  • An independent check on the record. The sixty published orbits we re-ran all repeat in our simulator too.

Fine print. Equal masses only. “Missing from the catalogue” means missing from that list, not necessarily unknown to everyone. The proof program is our own and has not been reviewed by others.
